\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{chap:Evaluation}

This chapter describes how the prototype is evaluated as an experimental sandbox for prism-effect research in VR. The purpose of the evaluation is not to test clinical rehabilitation on patients, but to assess how the implemented perturbations and input modalities behave in a controlled healthy-participant setup. The evaluation therefore focuses on whether the system can produce interpretable baseline, exposure, and post-exposure data across the implemented conditions, and whether participants can complete those conditions in a stable and comparable way.

\section{Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:evaluation-design}

The evaluation uses a mixed-methods approach within a mixed design. For clarity, the two modality conditions are defined as \emph{Condition 1: Controller} and \emph{Condition 2: Embodiment}. The between-subject grouping variable is therefore \emph{starting condition}: one group begins with controllers and the other begins with embodiment. The repeated within-subject factors are \emph{input mode}, with the levels \emph{controllers} and \emph{embodiment}, and \emph{effect mode}, with the levels \emph{translation}, \emph{rotation}, and \emph{skew}. As a result, every participant experiences all three implemented perturbations in both input modalities, but the order of the two modality blocks differs between groups.

This structure serves two purposes. First, it preserves the central comparison that motivates the project, namely whether the same prism-like perturbations behave differently when they are performed through controller input versus embodied hand tracking. Second, it reduces the risk that all modality-related differences are confounded with a fixed first-half versus second-half order effect. The design therefore treats modality order as its own grouping variable rather than allowing every participant to experience the prototype in the same sequence.

\section{Participants and Context}
\label{sec:evaluation-participants}

The evaluation is conducted with healthy adult participants recruited by convenience sampling. This fits the present stage of the project, where the immediate goal is to test the prototype as a configurable research sandbox before moving toward patient-facing use. Participants should have normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no known condition that would make VR exposure or repeated arm pointing inappropriate. Basic demographic information, handedness, and prior VR experience should be recorded so that the sample and its potential biases remain visible in the final analysis.

\section{Procedure}
\label{sec:evaluation-procedure}

Each participant completes one full session consisting of two modality blocks. Condition 1 uses controllers and Condition 2 uses embodied hand tracking. The participant's assigned group determines which of these two blocks comes first. Within each modality block, the participant experiences the three perturbation types: translation, rotation, and skew. Each effect is completed once in that modality before the participant moves on to the next effect.

Every effect condition follows the same overall prism-adaptation grammar implemented in the sandbox: a baseline measurement phase under \emph{no effect}, an exposure phase under the active perturbation, and a post-exposure repetition of the same measurement task. In other words, the manipulated condition is the perturbation and input mode, while the surrounding task structure remains stable across the run. This keeps the resulting sessions comparable even when the active effect changes.

Before the experimental conditions begin, the participant is introduced to the equipment, fitted with the headset, and calibrated inside the VR scene. A short familiarization period is then used so the participant can understand the current confirmation method and aiming behaviour without contributing data to the experimental blocks. This familiarization is especially important because the embodied condition uses dwell-based confirmation, whereas the controller condition can rely on trigger-based confirmation or the selected mitigation variant. After the participant completes the first condition block, a short questionnaire is administered before the modality switch. The same happens again after the second condition block, so each participant produces one questionnaire response per condition.

Within each modality block, the order of translation, rotation, and skew should be counterbalanced or randomized across participants. This reduces the risk that one specific effect is consistently advantaged or disadvantaged by fatigue, growing task familiarity, or residual carry-over from a previous perturbation. A short break should also be inserted between effect conditions, and the next condition should begin from a fresh baseline measurement rather than continuing directly from the previous post-exposure state.

At the level of one condition, the procedure is therefore repeated six times per participant: once for each combination of input mode and effect mode. The participant first completes the baseline block of the selected measurement task, then performs the exposure block under the chosen perturbation, and finally repeats the measurement block in post-exposure form. After the post-exposure block has finished, the system is reset to a neutral state before the next condition begins.

\section{Data Collection}
\label{sec:evaluation-data}

The evaluation adopts a mixed-methods approach by combining quantitative system logs with qualitative participant feedback. The quantitative part comes directly from the implemented logging pipeline. For each condition, the system stores metadata, accepted measurement trials, exposure attempts, target spawns, completion states, summary rows, and continuous movement samples. These data make it possible to inspect baseline performance, exposure behaviour, post-exposure change, spatial landing patterns, and detailed trajectories from the same participant session.

The qualitative part is intended to capture how participants experience the two input modalities and the three perturbation types. This includes one questionnaire after Condition 1, one questionnaire after Condition 2, and a final debrief or observation notes on hesitation, confusion, visible correction strategies, or fatigue. These qualitative accounts are useful because they provide context for interpreting the quantitative logs. For example, a condition with noisy exposure data may be easier to understand if participants also report uncertainty about dwell confirmation, unstable aiming, or difficulty perceiving the active shift.

\section{Evaluation Focus}
\label{sec:evaluation-focus}

The primary quantitative outcomes are the change from baseline to post-exposure and the behaviour of the exposure block itself. For the measurement phases, the important question is whether a condition produces an interpretable post-exposure shift relative to baseline. For the exposure phase, the important questions concern hit rate, target-relative error, trial-by-trial change, and overall stability. Because the sandbox also stores continuous samples, the evaluation can extend beyond endpoint measures and inspect whether different conditions produce visibly different movement traces, late corrections, or hovering behaviour.

At the comparison level, the evaluation asks whether the three perturbations behave similarly across the two input modalities and whether the modality order appears to matter. The between-subject grouping variable is therefore not treated as a nuisance, but as part of the evaluation structure. If controller-first and embodiment-first participants show different patterns, that difference can be interpreted as a possible sequence effect rather than being hidden inside the overall averages.

The qualitative analysis complements these quantitative comparisons by asking how participants describe the conditions. Relevant themes include perceived intuitiveness, bodily involvement, confirmation clarity, effort, fatigue, confidence in the aiming process, and whether one perturbation type feels more legible than another. Taken together, the mixed-methods evaluation therefore addresses both behavioural output and participant experience.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:evaluation-summary}

The evaluation is structured as a mixed-methods study with one between-subject grouping variable and two repeated within-subject factors. Group A starts with Condition 1, controllers, and Group B starts with Condition 2, embodiment, but both groups complete translation, rotation, and skew once in each modality. Each condition follows the same baseline--exposure--post-exposure structure, and each completed condition is followed by its own questionnaire. Together with the final debrief and the runtime logs, this makes the evaluation suitable for assessing the prototype as a comparative prism-effect sandbox rather than as a single-condition demonstration.
